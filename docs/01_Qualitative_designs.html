1  Qualitative designs

In this chapter, we compare quantitative and qualitative research and then practise the ideas about theory from this week’s lecture. These activities will help you make the shift from thinking in quantitative terms to thinking in qualitative terms.

Quantitative and qualitative research are two different ways of approaching research, and the data they produce is very different. As in the lecture, think of research methods as a toolbox: neither approach is better or easier than the other. You choose the tool that best fits the question you want to answer. The two approaches can also work well together.

Complete the activities below to practise the key differences between the two approaches (Activities 1 and 2) and the basics of theoretical perspectives (Activity 3).

1.1 Activity 1: Differences between qualitative and quantitative designs

Quantitative research generally… Qualitative research generally…
uses numbers as data uses meaning as data, usually expressed in words (e.g. interview transcripts), and sometimes images
is hypothesis-driven and deductive (‘top-down’): it starts with a theory, narrows it into hypotheses and tests them is exploratory, focused on research questions, and inductive (‘bottom-up’): it starts with observations, looks for patterns and builds ideas or theory from them
looks for precise, testable answers to specific questions (limits are set in advance) looks for rich, detailed knowledge of a topic area (no strict limits are set in advance)
focuses on cause and effect, with variables that are measured or manipulated focuses on experience: naturally occurring beliefs, behaviours and narratives
uses standardised, structured measures (e.g. questionnaires, assessments) uses flexible measures, often semi-structured (e.g. interviews, focus groups, diaries)
is often quicker to collect and analyse, because methods are standardised and are not changed part-way through is iterative and ongoing, and often takes longer; the focus can shift as participants raise unexpected ideas
focuses on generalisation: does what we find in a sample apply to a wider population? does not usually focus on generalisation, because the context in which data are gathered matters (findings can sometimes still contribute to wider understanding)
uses large samples (a power analysis estimates the minimum number), with data typically presented at group level uses small samples, with data that typically includes quotes from individuals; differences between participants are explored alongside patterns
is based on the belief that the researcher can aim for objectivity; the researcher’s values and experiences are seen as playing little or no part in the research process is based on the belief that the researcher’s subjective values and experiences are important; reflexivity is important and the researcher’s experiences are likely to influence the research process

This table describes general tendencies, not strict rules: notice the word ‘generally’ in the column headings (and on the lecture slides). Quantitative and qualitative approaches also complement each other. Activity 3 returns to the ideas behind some of these differences, such as what counts as knowledge and what role the researcher plays.

Look at the table above and write down answers for the following questions:

  • What might a ‘typical’ quantitative study look like? What kind of data might be collected? What would be the aim of the study? What might your sample look like?
  • What might a ‘typical’ qualitative study look like? What kind of data might be collected? What would be the aim of the study? What might your sample look like?
  • What type of research question might be best answered with a quantitative design?
  • What type of research question might be best answered with a qualitative design? (Think back to the ‘Why use qualitative methods?’ slide in the lecture.)

1.2 Activity 2: Research designs quiz

Choose whether a quantitative or qualitative design is most appropriate for each of the following studies. Why did you choose each option?

  1. Angela is conducting a study exploring the experiences of people currently experiencing mental ill health. She is interested in a) better understanding what people with mental ill health experience day-to-day, and b) exploring the support mechanisms that are in place

This study is more likely to be:

Explain the answer

This study would be best conducted as a qualitative study - the researcher is interested in exploring the experiences of people

  1. John is conducting a study where he is measuring whether the amount of sleep that children get is related to their cognitive performance in class. He asks the children to wear a sleep tracker to bed, so he can see when they are sleeping and when they are awake.

This study is more likely to be:

Explain the answer

This study would best be conducted as a quantitative study - the data will be quantified in numbers

  1. Sally is conducting a study where she is interested in how children and their parents make sense of the violence they see on TV.

This study is more likely to be:

Explain the answer

This study would best be conducted as a qualitative study – Sally is interested in how people understand and make sense of what they see (their beliefs and experiences), rather than in measuring a cause and its effect. The same topic could be studied quantitatively with a different research question. For example, if Sally asked whether the amount of violent TV that children watch predicts how aggressively they behave, she would be testing a relationship between measured variables, which is typical of quantitative research. The research question, not the topic, tells you which tool to pick.

  1. James is conducting a study where he wants to better understand motivations for smoking among teenagers.

This study is more likely to be:

Explain the answer

This study would probably best be conducted as a qualitative study - the researcher is looking at the motivations of the participants. However, it could also possibly be a quantitative study, if the motivations are measured and quantified on a numeric scale instead of text data.

  1. Elaine is conducting a study exploring whether the amount of studying done in preparation for an exam predicts exam performance.

This study is more likely to be:

Explain the answer

This study would be quantitative – Elaine is testing a specific, predefined question about the relationship between two measured variables (amount of studying and exam performance), and wants a precise, testable answer. Qualitative designs are not typically used to test relationships between measured variables.

1.3 Activity 3: Thinking about theory

In the lecture, you met two big questions that sit behind every research study (epistemology and ontology) and three types of knowledge that qualitative researchers can aim to produce (realist, phenomenological and social constructionist). This activity should take about 20 minutes.

Why does this matter? In the Method section of your qualitative report, you will briefly describe your theoretical approach (see Chapter 8). The aim here is to recognise the ideas and to be able to use them clearly.

1.3.1 Part A: Two big questions

  • Epistemology asks: how, and what, can we know?
  • Ontology asks: what is there to know?

For each statement below, decide whether it is mainly about ontology (what there is to know), epistemology (how, and what, we can know), or both. Choose your answer first, then open the explanation.

  1. ‘There is one single reality, and it exists whether or not anyone studies it.’

This statement is mainly about:

Explain the answer

Ontology. This is a claim about what exists and what reality is like. It is a realist position.

  1. ‘Reality depends on how people understand it, so there are multiple realities.’

This statement is mainly about:

Explain the answer

Ontology. Again, this is a claim about what exists: here, that there is more than one reality. It is a relativist position.

  1. ‘Researchers can produce objective knowledge by using observation, experiments and logic.’

This statement is mainly about:

Explain the answer

Epistemology. This is a claim about how we can know, and what counts as knowledge. It is the positivist idea that ‘the truth is out there’ to be uncovered by the researcher.

  1. ‘The researcher and the participants are connected, and the researcher’s values and experiences shape what the research finds.’

This statement is mainly about:

Explain the answer

Epistemology. This is a claim about how knowledge is produced. It is the constructionist idea that ‘as we change, truth changes’. It links to reflexivity: if the researcher helps to shape the findings, they need to be open about their own values (see Chapter 5).

  1. ‘There is a real world, but we can only ever know it partially, because our experiences and perspectives shape how we see it.’

This statement is mainly about:

Explain the answer

Both. The first half is about what exists (a reality exists), which is ontology. The second half is about what we can know of it (only partially), which is epistemology. This is critical realism, which sits between realism and relativism in the lecture.

  1. Spot the mix-up. A student says: ‘Anxiety cannot be measured perfectly, so it does not really exist.’ Which two questions have been run together?
Explain the answer

Whether something exists (ontology) and how well we can know it (epistemology). Something can exist and still be hard to know or to measure; a star that is too distant to detect is a simple example. Difficulty in measuring something does not show that it is not real.

1.3.2 Part B: One topic, three types of knowledge

Three researchers are all interested in homesickness among first-year international students (you will meet this topic again in Chapter 2). Each one wants to produce a different type of knowledge. Remember the three types and their images from the lecture:

  • Realist knowledge: the researcher as detective
  • Phenomenological knowledge: the researcher as person-centred counsellor
  • Social constructionist knowledge: the researcher as architect

Read each research question below. Which type of knowledge is it aiming to produce? Which words in the question gave you a clue?

  1. How is homesickness constructed in the way international students talk about missing home?

This research question aims to produce:

Explain the answer

Social constructionist (the architect). The question is about how students talk, not about what homesickness ‘really’ is. It assumes that missing home could be described in many ways, and asks how one version is built and what it does. Clue: ‘constructed in the way … talk’.

  1. What factors influence whether international students feel homesick during their first term?

This research question aims to produce:

Explain the answer

Realist (the detective). The question assumes that homesickness exists independently of the researcher’s and the participants’ views, and that its processes can be identified if the researcher asks the right questions. Clue: ‘what factors influence’.

  1. What is the lived experience of homesickness for international students in their first term?

This research question aims to produce:

Explain the answer

Phenomenological (the person-centred counsellor). The focus is on the participants’ own experience: how homesickness feels and what it means to them. The researcher is not trying to explain its causes or to check whether the experience matches an objective reality. Clue: ‘lived experience’.

These wordings are clues, not rules. Real research questions are often less tidy, and real studies often sit between these types. The three types also relate to the ideas in Part A, but the match is not exact, so there is no need to line them up perfectly.

Now apply it. Below is a short extract from an imaginary interview about homesickness, written for this activity. Imagine three researchers, each asking one of the research questions above, reading the same extract.

Honestly, I’m fine – it’s just the first few weeks. I don’t really have friends here yet, and I miss the little things, like my mum’s cooking and hearing everyone talking at home in the evenings. Everyone gets a bit homesick at the start, don’t they? I didn’t want to be the person who complains, so I just got on with it.

In one sentence each, write down what a realist, a phenomenological and a social constructionist researcher would pick out. There is no single correct answer: what matters is how your choice changes what you pay attention to.

Suggested answers
  • Realist: takes the account at face value, as a report of what happened and why. They would note possible factors in homesickness (few friends so far, missing family routines and food, the early weeks of term) and how the student coped (‘just got on with it’), looking for patterns across participants.
  • Phenomenological: is interested in the quality and meaning of the experience from the student’s point of view: the small, everyday details that make home feel absent (mum’s cooking, evening talk at home) and the feeling of not wanting to complain. They would not judge whether the student ‘should’ feel this way.
  • Social constructionist: assumes the experience could have been described in other ways, and asks what the language is achieving. ‘Honestly, I’m fine’ and ‘it’s just the first few weeks’ play down the problem; ‘Everyone gets a bit homesick … don’t they?’ presents it as normal and invites agreement; ‘I didn’t want to be the person who complains’ presents the speaker as someone who copes. The researcher would ask how this way of talking positions the student, and what it makes easy or difficult for others to say.
